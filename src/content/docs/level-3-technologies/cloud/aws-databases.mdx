## AWS Databases: Picking the Right Store for the Access Pattern

**AWS has more database services than most people can name, and interviewers rarely want a feature list. What they want is whether you can match a workload to a store and defend the choice: why DynamoDB rather than RDS for a high-write event log, why Redshift rather than Aurora for analytics, and what happens to your pipeline when you read from a production database at full speed during business hours.**

## Key Concepts

The organising distinction is **OLTP versus OLAP**. Transactional stores — RDS, Aurora, DynamoDB — are optimised for many small, fast reads and writes against individual rows, usually row-oriented and heavily indexed. Analytical stores — Redshift, and Athena over S3 — are columnar, optimised for scanning and aggregating billions of rows. Running analytics on a transactional database is the most common architectural mistake in this space, and it degrades the application that database exists to serve.

The second is **relational versus purpose-built**. AWS's stated philosophy is a database per workload: key-value (DynamoDB), document (DocumentDB), in-memory (ElastiCache), graph (Neptune), ledger (QLDB), relational (RDS/Aurora), warehouse (Redshift). In practice most teams need two or three, and the interview value is knowing which access pattern justifies leaving relational.

For a data engineer, the third idea is **how you get data out**. Reading from a production OLTP database with a full-table `SELECT` is disruptive. The mature answers are a **read replica**, a **snapshot export to S3**, or **CDC via DMS** — and knowing which to use when is a recurring interview question.

## RDS & Aurora

<QuestionList topic="RDS & Aurora" storagePrefix="aws-databases-rds">

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-rds-01" number={1} question="What is RDS and what does it manage for you?" difficulty="easy" topic="RDS">
Managed relational databases — PostgreSQL, MySQL, MariaDB, Oracle, SQL Server and Aurora.

AWS handles provisioning, patching, automated backups, point-in-time recovery, Multi-AZ failover, monitoring and read replicas. You keep responsibility for schema design, query performance, indexing and capacity choices.

What you give up versus self-managing on EC2: **no OS access**, so no custom agents or filesystem tuning, and only the extensions and parameters AWS exposes. For most teams that's a good trade — the operational burden RDS removes is substantial, and the restrictions rarely bind.

For a data engineer, RDS is usually the **source** system, not the platform: it's where the application's data lives, and your job is extracting from it without hurting it.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-rds-02" number={2} question="What is Aurora, and how does it differ from standard RDS?" difficulty="medium" topic="Aurora">
A cloud-native reimplementation of MySQL and PostgreSQL that keeps the wire protocol but replaces the storage engine entirely.

The key architectural difference: Aurora separates compute from a **distributed storage layer** that replicates six ways across three AZs and handles replication at the storage level. Consequences:

- **Faster** — commonly cited at 3–5× MySQL, 2–3× PostgreSQL.
- **Storage auto-scales** to 128 TB with no provisioning.
- **Up to 15 read replicas** sharing the same storage, so replica lag is typically milliseconds rather than seconds.
- **Fast crash recovery**, since redo is applied at the storage layer.
- **Backtrack** (MySQL) — rewind the cluster in place, without a restore.
- **Aurora Serverless v2** — scales capacity in fine-grained increments.

It costs more per hour than equivalent RDS, so the justification is performance, replica lag, or the operational features.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-rds-03" number={3} question="What is the difference between Multi-AZ and read replicas?" difficulty="medium" topic="RDS">
They solve different problems and are frequently confused.

**Multi-AZ** is for **availability**. A standby in another AZ receives synchronous replication and is **not readable**. On failure, AWS fails over automatically and the DNS endpoint points at the new primary. You're paying for resilience, not capacity.

**Read replicas** are for **read scaling**. They're asynchronous, readable, can be in other regions, and can be promoted manually. Because replication is async, they can **lag** — so a pipeline reading a replica may see stale data, which matters if you're joining against something written seconds ago.

For data engineering, **read replicas are the standard extraction target**: run your heavy analytical reads there, so a long scan doesn't compete with production traffic. Just account for lag in any freshness guarantee you make.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-rds-04" number={4} question="How do you extract data from RDS without impacting production?" difficulty="hard" topic="RDS">
Options, roughly in increasing order of sophistication:

1. **Query a read replica** — simplest, and sufficient for scheduled batch extraction. Watch replica lag.
2. **Export a snapshot to S3** — RDS can export an automated or manual snapshot directly to S3 as Parquet, with **zero load on the live database**. Excellent for full-table refreshes.
3. **DMS with CDC** — reads the transaction log (WAL/binlog) and streams changes with minimal overhead. The right answer for near-real-time replication.
4. **Aurora zero-ETL to Redshift** — a managed integration that continuously replicates into Redshift without you building a pipeline.
5. **Federated query** — Redshift or Athena querying RDS live. Convenient for small lookups, dangerous for large scans.

The bad answer is a nightly `SELECT *` against the primary during business hours. Snapshot export and CDC exist specifically to avoid it.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-rds-05" number={5} question="How do backups and point-in-time recovery work in RDS?" difficulty="medium" topic="RDS">
**Automated backups** take a daily snapshot plus continuous transaction log capture, retained 1–35 days, enabling **point-in-time recovery** to any second in that window. They're deleted when the instance is deleted (unless you take a final snapshot).

**Manual snapshots** persist until you delete them and can be shared or copied across accounts and regions.

Two details that matter operationally: a restore creates a **new instance** with a new endpoint — you can't restore in place, so cutover requires a DNS or config change. And Aurora's **Backtrack** is the exception, rewinding in place in minutes, which is genuinely useful after a bad migration.

For a data platform, snapshot copies to a separate account are the standard protection against a compromised or mistakenly-deleted production account.
</QuestionCard>

</QuestionList>

## DynamoDB

<QuestionList topic="DynamoDB" storagePrefix="aws-databases-dynamodb">

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-ddb-01" number={1} question="What is DynamoDB and when should you choose it?" difficulty="medium" topic="DynamoDB">
A fully managed, serverless key-value and document database with single-digit millisecond latency at effectively any scale, and no servers or capacity planning in the traditional sense.

Choose it when:
- Access patterns are **known and simple** — lookups by key, not ad-hoc queries.
- You need **predictable low latency** at high request rates.
- Write volume is very high — event ingestion, session state, IoT telemetry.
- You want **serverless operations** with automatic scaling.

Don't choose it when you need ad-hoc queries, complex joins, aggregations, or analytical scans. Those are exactly what it's bad at, and a `Scan` over a large table is slow, expensive, and consumes the capacity your application needs.

The mental model: DynamoDB is a **fast, infinitely scalable index** you design *around your queries*, not a general-purpose database you query however you like later.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-ddb-02" number={2} question="How do partition keys and sort keys work, and what is a hot partition?" difficulty="hard" topic="DynamoDB">
The **partition key** is hashed to choose a physical partition; the optional **sort key** orders items within it. Together they form the primary key, and a `Query` can efficiently fetch a range within one partition key.

A **hot partition** happens when traffic concentrates on one key value — partitioning events by `date` puts an entire day on one partition, which throttles regardless of how much capacity the table has, because per-partition throughput is bounded.

Fixes:
- **Choose a high-cardinality partition key** — `customer_id` rather than `status`.
- **Write sharding** — append a suffix (`2026-01-15#7`) to spread a hot key across N partitions, then query all N and merge.
- **Adaptive capacity** helps automatically but doesn't rescue a genuinely single-key hotspot.

This is the most common DynamoDB design failure and a very common interview question.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-ddb-03" number={3} question="What is the difference between a GSI and an LSI?" difficulty="medium" topic="DynamoDB">
| | **GSI** (Global Secondary Index) | **LSI** (Local Secondary Index) |
|---|---|---|
| Partition key | **different** from the base table | **same** as the base table |
| When created | any time | **only at table creation** |
| Consistency | eventually consistent only | strongly consistent available |
| Capacity | its own | shares the table's |
| Size limit | none | 10 GB per partition key |

**GSIs are what you actually use**: they enable a completely different access pattern, can be added later, and scale independently. The eventual consistency is rarely a problem for read paths.

**LSIs** are constrained by the create-time-only rule and the 10 GB limit, so most teams avoid them.

Note GSIs cost extra writes — every base-table write that changes indexed attributes also writes to each GSI, so ten indexes means roughly ten times the write cost.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-ddb-04" number={4} question="Explain DynamoDB capacity modes." difficulty="medium" topic="DynamoDB">
**Provisioned** — you specify read and write capacity units, optionally with auto-scaling. Cheapest for steady, predictable traffic, and eligible for reserved capacity discounts. Exceeding capacity causes throttling.

**On-Demand** — pay per request, scales instantly, no planning. Roughly 5–7× the per-request cost of well-utilised provisioned capacity, but far cheaper than provisioned capacity sitting idle.

The decision: **On-Demand for unpredictable, spiky, or new workloads** (you don't yet know the pattern) and for anything with a low duty cycle. **Provisioned with auto-scaling for steady, high-volume** production traffic where the savings are real.

You can switch modes, though only once every 24 hours. Many teams start On-Demand, observe the pattern for a month, then move to provisioned.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-ddb-05" number={5} question="How do you get data out of DynamoDB for analytics?" difficulty="hard" topic="DynamoDB">
Not with `Scan` — it's slow, consumes table capacity, and competes with production.

Better options:
1. **Export to S3** — a point-in-time export straight to S3 (DynamoDB JSON or Ion) with **zero consumed capacity**. The standard answer for full-table analytics.
2. **DynamoDB Streams + Lambda/Kinesis** — a change stream of item-level modifications, for incremental replication into the lake.
3. **Kinesis Data Streams for DynamoDB** — the same change data into Kinesis, then Firehose to S3.
4. **Glue connector** — reads with a configurable capacity percentage so you can bound the impact.
5. **Zero-ETL to Redshift** — a managed continuous integration.

The design principle worth stating: DynamoDB is an **operational** store. Analytics belongs in S3 or Redshift, fed by exports or streams — not by scanning the serving table.
</QuestionCard>

</QuestionList>

## Caching: ElastiCache & MemoryDB

<QuestionList topic="Caching: ElastiCache & MemoryDB" storagePrefix="aws-databases-caching">

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-cache-01" number={1} question="What is ElastiCache, and how do Redis and Memcached differ?" difficulty="medium" topic="ElastiCache">
Managed in-memory caching, in two engines.

**Memcached** — a simple multi-threaded key-value cache. No persistence, no replication, no rich data types. Scales by adding nodes and sharding client-side. Good when you want a plain, fast cache.

**Redis** — richer: lists, sets, sorted sets, hashes, streams, pub/sub, Lua scripting; supports replication, automatic failover, snapshots and cluster mode.

**Redis is the usual choice** because the extra capability costs nothing meaningful and the replication and persistence options matter as soon as a cache becomes load-bearing.

For data engineering, Redis appears as a **lookup cache** in enrichment pipelines (avoiding a database round trip per record), a **dedup set** for exactly-once-ish processing, and occasionally a **rate limiter** in front of an API.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-cache-02" number={2} question="What is MemoryDB, and how is it different from ElastiCache for Redis?" difficulty="medium" topic="MemoryDB">
MemoryDB is a **Redis-compatible durable database**, not a cache. It writes to a multi-AZ transaction log, giving **durability and strong consistency on primary reads**, with microsecond reads and single-digit millisecond writes.

ElastiCache for Redis is a **cache**: data loss on failure is acceptable because the source of truth is elsewhere. Its persistence options are backups, not a durability guarantee.

So the choice is about whether Redis is your **system of record**. If losing the data means losing information — a session store you can't rebuild, a real-time leaderboard that is the truth — MemoryDB. If it's a cache in front of a database, ElastiCache, which is cheaper.

Most data pipelines want ElastiCache; MemoryDB shows up when someone needs Redis semantics with durability.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-cache-03" number={3} question="What caching strategies should you know?" difficulty="medium" topic="Caching">
- **Cache-aside (lazy loading)** — the application checks the cache, and on a miss reads the database and populates it. Only requested data is cached, but every miss pays the full latency, and stale data persists until evicted.
- **Write-through** — write to cache and database together. The cache is always fresh; writes are slower, and you cache data that may never be read.
- **Write-behind** — write to the cache and flush to the database asynchronously. Fast writes, risk of loss.
- **Read-through** — the cache itself loads on a miss, hiding the pattern from the application.

Combine cache-aside with a **TTL** as the default: it bounds staleness without needing explicit invalidation, which is the hard part.

The classic failure is a **cache stampede** — a popular key expires and a thousand requests hit the database simultaneously. Mitigate with jittered TTLs, a lock so one request repopulates, or refreshing slightly before expiry.
</QuestionCard>

</QuestionList>

## Purpose-Built Databases

<QuestionList topic="Purpose-Built Databases" storagePrefix="aws-databases-purpose-built">

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-pb-01" number={1} question="What is DocumentDB and when would you use it?" difficulty="easy" topic="DocumentDB">
A managed document database with **MongoDB API compatibility**, storing JSON-like documents with flexible schemas. It separates compute from a distributed storage layer, like Aurora, and scales to 15 read replicas.

Use it when you have an existing MongoDB workload you want managed, or genuinely document-shaped data with variable structure and query patterns that suit document indexes.

The caveat to name: **it is not MongoDB** — it implements a subset of the API, and version and feature gaps have historically caught teams out. Check the specific operators and features your application needs before committing.

For new work on AWS, DynamoDB is often the better fit unless you specifically want document queries or MongoDB compatibility.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-pb-02" number={2} question="What is Neptune, and what problems suit a graph database?" difficulty="medium" topic="Neptune">
A managed graph database supporting **property graphs** (Gremlin, openCypher) and **RDF** (SPARQL).

Graph databases win when the **relationships are the query**: fraud rings, "customers who bought this also bought", entity resolution across sources, network and dependency topology, recommendation traversal, and knowledge graphs.

The technical reason: a multi-hop traversal in SQL becomes a chain of self-joins whose cost explodes with depth. A graph engine stores adjacency directly, so traversal cost scales with the results, not the table size.

For data engineering specifically, the realistic uses are **data lineage** and **entity resolution** — both naturally graph-shaped. It's a specialised tool, so justify it with a traversal-heavy access pattern rather than because the data "has relationships."
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-pb-03" number={3} question="What is QLDB and when is it appropriate?" difficulty="medium" topic="QLDB">
A ledger database with an **immutable, append-only journal** that is cryptographically verifiable — you can prove a record's history hasn't been altered.

Fits where an audit trail must be provable rather than merely present: financial transaction records, regulatory compliance, supply chain provenance, and systems of record where "who changed what, when, and has it been tampered with" is a formal requirement.

The distinction from just adding an audit table: an audit table can be modified by anyone with write access. QLDB's journal is append-only and hash-chained, so tampering is detectable.

Worth knowing that **AWS has announced QLDB's deprecation**, recommending Aurora PostgreSQL with a verifiable ledger approach instead — so mention it as a concept rather than proposing it for a new build.
</QuestionCard>

</QuestionList>

## Redshift

<QuestionList topic="Redshift" storagePrefix="aws-databases-redshift">

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-rs-01" number={1} question="What is Redshift and how is its architecture different from RDS?" difficulty="medium" topic="Redshift">
A petabyte-scale **columnar MPP data warehouse**. A leader node parses and plans; compute nodes hold slices of the data and execute in parallel.

Differences from a row-store like RDS:
- **Columnar storage** — a query touching 3 of 200 columns reads only those, and per-column compression is far more effective.
- **MPP** — work is distributed across node slices and executed in parallel.
- **No indexes** — distribution and sort keys replace them.
- **Optimised for scans and aggregations**, not point lookups or high-concurrency single-row writes.

**RA3 nodes** separate compute from **managed storage in S3**, so you scale them independently and pay for what you use — a significant change from the older DC2 model where storage was tied to node count.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-rs-02" number={2} question="What are distribution and sort keys, and how do you choose them?" difficulty="hard" topic="Redshift">
**Distribution style** decides which node slice each row lands on:
- **KEY** — rows with the same value co-locate. Set it on the **join column of large fact-to-dimension joins** so the join happens locally rather than redistributing data across the network.
- **ALL** — a full copy on every node. Right for **small dimension tables**, since it makes every join to them local.
- **EVEN** — round-robin. The fallback when there's no dominant join key.
- **AUTO** — Redshift chooses and can change it as the table grows. A reasonable default now.

**Sort keys** determine physical row order, letting Redshift skip blocks using zone-map min/max values. Choose the column you **filter on most**, usually a date.

The failure mode to name is **distribution skew**: a KEY distribution on a low-cardinality or skewed column puts most rows on one slice, so one node does all the work.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-rs-03" number={3} question="How do you load data into Redshift efficiently?" difficulty="medium" topic="Redshift">
**`COPY` from S3** is the answer — it loads in parallel across all slices, which single-row `INSERT`s cannot.

```sql
COPY orders FROM 's3://lake/curated/orders/'
IAM_ROLE 'arn:aws:iam::123456789012:role/redshift-load'
FORMAT AS PARQUET;
```

Practices that matter:
- **Split input into multiple files**, ideally a multiple of the slice count, so every slice works.
- **Prefer Parquet or gzipped files**; avoid a single huge uncompressed file, which serialises the load.
- **Never load row by row** — `INSERT` per record is orders of magnitude slower.
- **`ANALYZE`** after significant loads so the planner has current statistics.
- **Use staging tables** and swap, so consumers never see a partial load.

`UNLOAD` is the reverse, writing query results to S3 in parallel — the standard way to push data back into the lake.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-rs-04" number={4} question="What is Redshift Spectrum?" difficulty="medium" topic="Redshift">
It lets Redshift query data **in S3** directly, without loading it, using external tables defined in the Glue Data Catalog. A single query can join a Spectrum external table to a native Redshift table.

Why it matters architecturally: you keep hot, frequently-joined data in Redshift for speed and leave cold historical data in S3, querying across both. That avoids loading years of history you rarely touch.

Pricing is **per terabyte scanned**, like Athena — so the same optimisations apply: partition the data, use Parquet, and select only needed columns. An unpartitioned Spectrum table scanned repeatedly gets expensive quickly.

It's slower than native Redshift storage, so it complements the warehouse rather than replacing it.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="awsdb-rs-05" number={5} question="What is Redshift Serverless and when would you choose it?" difficulty="medium" topic="Redshift">
Redshift without provisioned clusters: you set a base capacity in **RPUs** and a maximum, and it scales automatically, charging per second of use with no cost when idle.

Choose it for **intermittent or unpredictable** workloads — a warehouse used heavily during business hours and idle overnight, development environments, or a new project where you can't yet size a cluster.

Choose **provisioned** for steady, high-utilisation workloads, where reserved-instance pricing is substantially cheaper than serverless RPU-seconds, and where you want fine control over node type and WLM.

The practical caution: serverless can be more expensive than expected if a badly-tuned query pattern keeps scaling capacity up. Set the maximum RPU limit deliberately, and monitor usage rather than assuming it's automatically cheaper.
</QuestionCard>

</QuestionList>

## Common Interview Scenarios

**"Your nightly extract from the production database is slowing the application."** — move to a read replica, or better, snapshot export to S3 for full loads and DMS CDC for incremental. Explain that the primary should never serve analytical scans.

**"Design storage for an event ingestion system at 50,000 writes per second."** — DynamoDB with a high-cardinality partition key, On-Demand capacity initially, DynamoDB Streams or Kinesis feeding S3 for analytics. Address hot partitions explicitly, since that's the failure the question is probing.

**"Queries against your Redshift cluster got slow after a data growth spurt."** — check distribution skew and whether the sort key still matches the filter pattern, look for unsorted regions needing `VACUUM`, confirm statistics are current, and check whether concurrency is queueing in WLM.

**"Why not just use RDS for everything?"** — it works until the access pattern diverges: analytical scans need columnar and MPP, extreme write throughput needs a distributed key-value store, and sub-millisecond lookups need memory. Say plainly that a single Postgres is the right answer more often than AWS's marketing suggests, and the burden of proof is on leaving it.
