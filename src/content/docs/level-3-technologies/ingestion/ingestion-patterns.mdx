## Data Ingestion Patterns: Choosing How Data Arrives

**"Should this be batch or streaming?" is one of the most common design questions in a data engineering interview, and the expected answer is almost never "streaming, obviously". Streaming costs more, is harder to reason about, and is harder to debug — so the strong answer starts by asking what latency the business actually needs, and only then picks a pattern. Candidates who reach for Kafka before asking that question are giving away the wrong signal.**

## Key Concepts

The patterns sit on a **latency spectrum**, and each step down costs money and complexity: **batch** (hours to days), **micro-batch** (seconds to minutes), **streaming** (sub-second to seconds), and **real-time** in the strict sense (guaranteed millisecond response, which is an operational-systems property rather than an analytics one).

The distinction that matters technically is **bounded versus unbounded** data. A batch job knows its input is finite — it can sort, aggregate globally, and finish. A streaming job processes an unbounded sequence, so "the total" is never final, which is why streaming needs windows, watermarks and a decision about what to do with late data. That's the real source of streaming's difficulty, not the infrastructure.

The practical framing for interviews: **latency requirement drives the pattern, not the other way round.** A daily report needs batch. A fraud check needs streaming. Most things in between are served well by micro-batch, which is why Spark Structured Streaming and hourly incremental loads cover so much real-world ground.

## Batch Ingestion

<QuestionList topic="Batch Ingestion" storagePrefix="ingestion-batch">

<QuestionCard id="ing-batch-01" number={1} question="What is batch ingestion, and when is it the right choice?" difficulty="easy" topic="Batch">
Processing data in bounded chunks on a schedule — hourly, daily, or triggered by arrival of a complete file.

It's the right choice when:
- **Consumers don't need fresher data.** A daily executive report gains nothing from second-level latency.
- **The source is naturally batch** — a partner's nightly file drop, a monthly billing extract.
- **The computation needs the whole set** — global ranking, deduplication across everything, a full reconciliation.
- **Cost matters** — batch uses ephemeral compute that scales to zero between runs, where streaming pays for always-on infrastructure.
- **Simplicity matters** — reruns are trivial, debugging is straightforward, and backfills are just another run.

The honest default: **batch unless there's a stated latency requirement that batch can't meet.** It's cheaper, simpler, easier to test and far easier to operate, and a large proportion of "we need streaming" turns out to mean "we need hourly".
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="ing-batch-02" number={2} question="What are the common batch ingestion patterns?" difficulty="medium" topic="Batch">
- **Full snapshot** — copy everything each run and replace the target. Simple and self-healing; a bad run is corrected by the next one. Impractical past a certain volume.
- **Incremental by watermark** — pull rows where `updated_at` exceeds the last high-water mark, then advance it after success. Cheap; **misses deletes**, and depends on the source maintaining that column honestly.
- **Partition overwrite** — re-process and replace whole partitions (usually a date). Idempotent by construction, which is why it's the workhorse of most lakes.
- **Append-only** — add new immutable records, never update. Ideal for event data.
- **Snapshot plus CDC** — an initial bulk load, then change capture keeps it current.

The pattern to default to is **partition overwrite**: re-running a date replaces rather than duplicates, so retries and backfills are safe without any extra machinery.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="ing-batch-03" number={3} question="How do you handle late-arriving data in batch?" difficulty="hard" topic="Batch">
Data for Monday can arrive on Wednesday — a partner resends a corrected file, a mobile client uploads after being offline, an upstream job reruns.

The approaches:
- **Partition by event date, not ingestion date**, and re-process the affected partition when late data appears. Correct, and the reason partition overwrite matters.
- **A rolling reprocess window** — every run also reprocesses the last N days, so late arrivals are absorbed automatically at bounded cost.
- **Two timestamps on every row** — `event_time` and `ingested_at` — so consumers can distinguish "when it happened" from "when we learned about it", and so a reconciliation can find rows that arrived late.
- **Late-arrival alerting**, so a source that starts arriving days late is visible rather than silently distorting recent numbers.

The failure to avoid is partitioning by **ingestion** date and reporting on it — then Monday's late data appears in Wednesday's numbers and both days are wrong, with nothing erroring.
</QuestionCard>

</QuestionList>

## Streaming Ingestion

<QuestionList topic="Streaming Ingestion" storagePrefix="ingestion-streaming">

<QuestionCard id="ing-str-01" number={1} question="What is streaming ingestion, and what does it actually require?" difficulty="medium" topic="Streaming">
Continuous processing of an unbounded sequence of events as they arrive, typically through a durable log — Kafka, Kinesis, Pub/Sub, Event Hubs — with consumers processing and committing offsets.

What it requires beyond a message bus:
- **A decision about time** — event time versus processing time, and windows to make an unbounded stream aggregable.
- **Watermarks and a late-data policy**, because events arrive out of order.
- **Idempotent sinks**, because delivery is at-least-once.
- **Checkpointing** so a restart resumes rather than replays everything.
- **Always-on infrastructure**, monitored continuously, with on-call implications a nightly batch job doesn't have.

The point worth making: the message bus is the easy part. The hard parts are **correctness under out-of-order and late data**, and the operational burden of a system that's never idle.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="ing-str-02" number={2} question="What are the delivery semantics, and which can you actually get?" difficulty="hard" topic="Streaming">
- **At-most-once** — send and forget. Fast, and messages can be lost. Acceptable only for disposable telemetry.
- **At-least-once** — retry until acknowledged. **No loss, but duplicates.** The default nearly everywhere.
- **Exactly-once** — each message affects the result once. Strictly impossible across a network in the general case.

What "exactly-once" means in practice is **effectively-once**: at-least-once delivery combined with **idempotent processing**, so duplicates don't change the outcome. Mechanisms: a deterministic key with an upsert or `MERGE`, a dedup table keyed on `stream+partition+offset`, or transactional checkpointing where the offset commit and the output write are atomic (Flink checkpoints, Kafka transactions).

The strong answer states plainly that you **design the sink to be idempotent** rather than trying to make delivery perfect — because retries, restarts and replays will happen regardless.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="ing-str-03" number={3} question="What is backpressure, and how do you handle it?" difficulty="hard" topic="Streaming">
When a downstream stage can't keep up with the rate arriving, work accumulates. Unhandled, it means growing memory, growing lag, and eventually a crash or data loss.

Handling it:
- **Pull-based consumption** — the consumer requests what it can handle, which is why Kafka and Kinesis consumers pull rather than being pushed to. This is backpressure by design.
- **Buffering in the durable log** — the broker's retention absorbs bursts, which is a large part of why you put a log in front of processing at all.
- **Autoscaling consumers** on lag, not CPU. Consumer lag is the correct signal.
- **Rate limiting or load shedding** where dropping is acceptable.
- **Framework-level backpressure** — Flink propagates it upstream automatically; Spark's `maxOffsetsPerTrigger` bounds each micro-batch.

The metric to monitor is **consumer lag**, and the alert should be on lag *trend*, not absolute value — steadily growing lag means you'll never catch up, which is the real failure.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="ing-str-04" number={4} question="When is streaming genuinely the right answer?" difficulty="medium" topic="Streaming">
When the **value of the data decays in seconds or minutes**:

- **Fraud and anomaly detection** — a decision after the transaction completes is worthless.
- **Real-time personalisation** — recommendations reacting to the current session.
- **Operational monitoring and alerting** on live systems.
- **Dynamic pricing and inventory** where stale numbers cause overselling.
- **IoT and telemetry** where the volume makes batching impractical anyway.
- **Event-driven microservice integration**, where the stream is the integration mechanism rather than an analytics choice.

And when it isn't: daily and weekly reporting, month-end finance, most ML training, and anything where a human looks at the number once a day.

The question to ask an interviewer — and a genuinely good move — is **"what decision is made with this data, and how quickly?"** That converts a technology debate into a requirements one.
</QuestionCard>

</QuestionList>

## Real-Time & Micro-Batch

<QuestionList topic="Real-Time & Micro-Batch" storagePrefix="ingestion-realtime">

<QuestionCard id="ing-rt-01" number={1} question="What is micro-batch, and why is it so widely used?" difficulty="medium" topic="Micro-Batch">
Processing small batches on a short interval — seconds to minutes — so it looks continuous while retaining batch mechanics. Spark Structured Streaming works this way by default, as do frequent incremental loads.

Why it's the pragmatic middle:
- **Batch semantics** — exactly-once through atomic commits, simple reruns, familiar debugging.
- **Better throughput per record** than event-at-a-time, because per-record overhead is amortised.
- **The same code and engine** as your batch pipelines, so one skill set and one codebase.
- **Latency of seconds**, which satisfies the large majority of "real-time" requirements once you ask what they actually mean.

The trade is a latency floor set by the batch interval, and slightly more end-to-end delay than a true event-at-a-time engine like Flink. For most analytics that's irrelevant, which is why micro-batch quietly covers a huge share of production "streaming".
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="ing-rt-02" number={2} question="What does 'real-time' actually mean, and how is it different from streaming?" difficulty="medium" topic="Real-Time">
Strictly, **real-time** means a guaranteed response within a bounded time — a hard deadline, as in control systems. Missing the deadline is a failure, not a delay.

In data engineering the word is used loosely to mean "fast", which causes genuine confusion in requirements gathering. Worth separating:

- **Near real-time** — seconds. What most "real-time dashboards" actually need.
- **Streaming** — continuous processing; latency is typically low but not *guaranteed*.
- **Hard real-time** — a bounded guarantee. Analytics systems don't provide this, and shouldn't claim to.

The useful interview move is to **ask what latency the decision requires** rather than accepting "real-time" as a specification. Very often "real-time" means "not tomorrow", and an hourly micro-batch satisfies it at a fraction of the cost and complexity.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="ing-rt-03" number={3} question="How do you serve low-latency queries over streaming data?" difficulty="hard" topic="Real-Time">
Processing fast isn't enough — the serving layer must also answer quickly, and a warehouse usually can't at single-digit milliseconds.

Options:
- **A key-value serving store** — DynamoDB, Bigtable, Redis — written by the stream processor, holding current state for point lookups.
- **A real-time OLAP engine** — Apache Druid, Pinot, ClickHouse, or Azure Data Explorer — built for sub-second aggregation over recent high-cardinality data.
- **Materialised views** maintained incrementally by the stream job.
- **A hybrid**: recent data in the fast store, history in the warehouse, unioned at query time.

The architectural point: **the processing pattern and the serving pattern are separate decisions.** Streaming ingestion into a warehouse that takes ten seconds to answer hasn't delivered a real-time product — you need a serving layer designed for the query latency you promised.
</QuestionCard>

</QuestionList>

## Hybrid Architectures

<QuestionList topic="Hybrid Architectures" storagePrefix="ingestion-hybrid">

<QuestionCard id="ing-hyb-01" number={1} question="What is the Lambda architecture?" difficulty="hard" topic="Hybrid">
Two parallel paths over the same data:

- **Batch layer** — reprocesses the complete historical dataset periodically, producing accurate, authoritative results.
- **Speed layer** — processes the live stream for low-latency approximate results covering the recent window.
- **Serving layer** — merges both, so queries get accurate history plus fresh recent data.

It solved a real problem when streaming engines couldn't be trusted for correctness: the batch layer guaranteed eventual accuracy while the speed layer provided freshness.

Its well-known cost is **maintaining the same business logic twice**, in two codebases and often two languages. They drift, and reconciling batch and streaming numbers that should match becomes a recurring tax. That duplication is precisely what Kappa proposed to remove — and why Lambda is now more a concept to explain than an architecture to propose.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="ing-hyb-02" number={2} question="What is the Kappa architecture, and did it win?" difficulty="hard" topic="Hybrid">
**One path**: everything is a stream. The durable log retains history, and "reprocessing" means replaying the log through a new version of the streaming job. No separate batch layer, no duplicated logic.

It requires a log with long or infinite retention, a stream processor whose semantics you trust for correctness, and enough throughput to replay history in reasonable time.

Did it win? **Partly.** The insight — don't maintain two implementations — was correct and is now standard. But few teams run pure Kappa, because replaying years of history through a streaming job is often slower and more expensive than a batch job over columnar files.

What actually emerged is a **third thing**: a lakehouse where the same engine and the same code run in both batch and streaming modes over the same tables — Spark Structured Streaming or Flink writing Delta or Iceberg. You get Kappa's single codebase with batch's efficiency for reprocessing, which is the practical answer to give.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="ing-hyb-03" number={3} question="What is a hybrid ingestion approach in practice?" difficulty="medium" topic="Hybrid">
Most real platforms are hybrid, not by design purity but because different sources have different needs:

- **CDC streaming** from operational databases for freshness on core entities.
- **Batch file ingestion** from partners who send daily drops, because that's what they send.
- **Event streaming** for clickstream and telemetry.
- **Scheduled API pulls** for third-party SaaS, because their rate limits make streaming impossible.

They converge on a **common landing zone** in an open table format, after which everything downstream is uniform — one transformation layer, one serving layer, regardless of how data arrived.

That convergence is the design point worth articulating: **vary the ingestion pattern per source, standardise everything after it.** Trying to force every source into one pattern produces either unnecessary streaming infrastructure or unnecessarily stale core data.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="ing-hyb-04" number={4} question="How do you decide the ingestion pattern for a new source?" difficulty="medium" topic="Hybrid">
Work through five questions, in order:

1. **What decision does this data drive, and how fast?** This sets the latency requirement, and it's the only question that genuinely determines the pattern.
2. **What can the source actually produce?** A partner's nightly SFTP drop cannot be streamed, whatever you'd prefer.
3. **What volume and shape?** Very high-volume telemetry may need streaming simply because batching it is impractical.
4. **Do you need deletes and intermediate states?** That points to CDC over a timestamp-based pull.
5. **What can the team operate?** Streaming infrastructure needs on-call coverage; a team of two may be better served by hourly batch that they can actually maintain.

Then default to the **simplest pattern that meets the latency requirement**. The strong candidate reaches for streaming only when questions 1 or 3 force it, and says so explicitly.
</QuestionCard>

</QuestionList>

## Common Interview Scenarios

**"Should we build this as batch or streaming?"** — ask what decision the data drives and how quickly. If nobody acts on it within the hour, batch. Volunteering that streaming costs more in infrastructure, complexity and on-call is the part that signals experience.

**"Design ingestion for a system with a database, a clickstream and partner files."** — hybrid by source: CDC for the database, a streaming log for clickstream, event-triggered batch for the files, all converging on one landing zone in an open table format so everything downstream is uniform.

**"Your streaming numbers don't match the batch reconciliation."** — late data dropped beyond allowed lateness, processing-time windows where event-time was needed, or duplicates from at-least-once delivery into a non-idempotent sink. All three are worth checking in that order.

**"Explain Lambda vs Kappa."** — Lambda duplicates logic across batch and speed layers, which is its fatal cost; Kappa unifies on a replayable log. Then note that what actually won is a lakehouse running the same code in both modes over the same tables.
