## Data Observability: Monitoring and Incident Response

**Data observability is the practice of monitoring, alerting, and diagnosing the health of data and data pipelines — catching silent failures before they reach a dashboard, and cutting the time it takes to find root cause once something breaks.**

## Key Concepts

Data observability differs from application observability in what it watches: instead of latency and error rates for request/response services, it tracks dataset health over time — **freshness** (is data arriving on schedule), **volume** (are row/byte/partition counts within expected bounds), **schema** (did columns get added, removed, or retyped), and **quality** (nulls, uniqueness, referential integrity). These signals matter because data failures are often silent — a job succeeds, but the data is wrong, missing a subset, or duplicated — so detection depends on dataset-level checks and reconciliation rather than job exit codes alone. When something breaks, **lineage** tells you the blast radius (which downstream tables and dashboards are affected), and a good **runbook** turns an alert into a repeatable recovery process rather than a from-scratch investigation.

## Interview Questions

<QuestionList topic="Observability" storagePrefix="observability">

<QuestionCard id="obs-01" number={1} question="What is data observability?" difficulty="easy" topic="Fundamentals">
Data observability is the practice of monitoring, alerting, and diagnosing the health of data and data pipelines. It focuses on whether datasets are arriving on time, with correct volume and values, and whether downstream consumers can rely on them. The goal is to reduce "data downtime" and speed up root-cause analysis.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="obs-02" number={2} question="How is data observability different from application observability?" difficulty="easy" topic="Fundamentals">
Application observability focuses on service health (latency, error rates, traces) for request/response systems. Data observability focuses on dataset health and pipeline behavior over time (freshness, completeness, distribution shifts). Data issues can be silent, delayed, and cumulative, so you need different signals and baselines.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="obs-03" number={3} question="What are the key signals you monitor for data pipelines?" difficulty="medium" topic="Signals">
Common signals include:

- **Freshness** — track max event/ingestion time to ensure data is arriving on schedule
- **Volume** — monitor row counts, bytes, and partitions for anomalies
- **Schema changes** — detect added, removed, or typed columns that may break downstream
- **Quality checks** — validate null rates, uniqueness, and referential integrity
- **Pipeline run status** — track job success/failure and execution duration
- **Cost signals** — monitor query time, bytes scanned, and cluster utilization
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="obs-04" number={4} question="How do you define and measure an end-to-end SLA for data?" difficulty="medium" topic="SLAs">
You define the SLA from source event time to availability in the final dataset or BI metric. Measure it with timestamps at each stage (ingest, transform, publish) and compute percentiles (p50/p95) as well as breach counts. SLAs should reflect consumer needs and include ownership and escalation paths.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="obs-05" number={5} question="What should you log for each pipeline run?" difficulty="easy" topic="Logging">
At minimum:

- **Inputs** — source tables/partitions, offsets, watermarks
- **Outputs** — target tables/partitions, row counts written
- **Runtime and resource usage** — CPU, memory, execution time
- **Warnings/errors** — retry attempts, failure details
- **Data quality results** — summary of validation checks

This makes runs reproducible and speeds up debugging.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="obs-06" number={6} question="How do you detect silent failures?" difficulty="hard" topic="Failure Detection">
Silent failures happen when the job succeeds but data is wrong (missing subset, wrong join, truncation). Detect them with dataset-level checks: freshness, volume bounds, distribution checks, and reconciliations against sources. Monitoring key business KPIs for unexpected changes is also effective.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="obs-07" number={7} question="What is lineage and how does it help during incidents?" difficulty="medium" topic="Lineage">
Lineage describes upstream/downstream dependencies between datasets, jobs, and dashboards. During incidents, lineage helps you assess blast radius (which reports are impacted) and prioritize fixes. It also helps prevent regressions by making dependencies explicit.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="obs-08" number={8} question="How do you monitor and debug a broken metric in BI?" difficulty="hard" topic="Incident Response">
You start from the metric definition and trace dependencies to the underlying tables. Then check data freshness and recent changes (deploys, schema changes, upstream outages). Use reconciliation queries and sampling to locate where the metric diverged, then confirm whether it is a data issue or a definition change.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="obs-09" number={9} question="How do you design alerts to avoid alert fatigue?" difficulty="medium" topic="Alerting">
Alerts should be actionable, scoped, and owned. Use severity levels, suppression windows, and baselines that account for seasonality. Prefer multi-signal alerts (freshness + volume) over single noisy checks, and include runbooks with clear next steps.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="obs-10" number={10} question="How do you approach backfills safely?" difficulty="hard" topic="Backfills">
Backfills can be expensive and risky because they rewrite history and can change metrics. A safe approach includes running in smaller batches, validating each batch, and separating "raw" from "published" layers so you can rebuild derived tables without re-ingesting sources. You also communicate expected metric changes and schedule around peak usage.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="obs-11" number={11} question="What is a runbook and what should it contain?" difficulty="medium" topic="Runbooks">
A runbook is a step-by-step guide for responding to an alert or incident. It should include impact assessment, common root causes, validation queries, mitigation steps, rollback/backfill steps, and escalation contacts. Good runbooks reduce time-to-recovery for on-call engineers.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="obs-12" number={12} question="What are common incident patterns in data platforms?" difficulty="medium" topic="Incident Patterns">
Common patterns include:

- **Upstream outages** — late or missing data from source systems
- **Schema changes** — breaking transforms when columns are added/removed/changed
- **Duplicates** — data duplication from retries or reprocessing
- **Incorrect joins** — metric inflation due to join logic errors
- **Partition/timezone bugs** — incorrect data filtering due to boundary issues
- **Cost spikes** — expensive queries consuming excessive resources
</QuestionCard>

</QuestionList>

## Metrics & Prometheus

<QuestionList topic="Metrics & Prometheus" storagePrefix="observability-prometheus">

<QuestionCard id="obsv-prom-01" number={1} question="What is Prometheus and how does it collect metrics?" difficulty="medium" topic="Prometheus">
An open-source metrics database and alerting system, and the de facto standard in the Kubernetes ecosystem.

Its defining choice is **pull-based scraping**: Prometheus periodically fetches a `/metrics` HTTP endpoint from each target rather than receiving pushes. Targets are found via **service discovery** (Kubernetes, Consul, cloud APIs), so a new pod is scraped automatically without configuration.

Data is stored as **time series** identified by a metric name plus labels, and queried with **PromQL**.

Pull has real advantages — you can tell whether a target is *down* (a failed scrape) rather than merely silent, and there's no client-side buffering to lose. For short-lived batch jobs that exit before a scrape, the **Pushgateway** exists as the exception, though it's discouraged for anything else.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="obsv-prom-02" number={2} question="What are the Prometheus metric types?" difficulty="medium" topic="Prometheus">
- **Counter** — monotonically increasing; resets to zero on restart. For totals: rows processed, errors, bytes written. Always query it with `rate()` or `increase()`, never raw.
- **Gauge** — goes up and down. For current state: queue depth, memory in use, lag in seconds.
- **Histogram** — observations bucketed by value, plus a sum and count. For latency and size distributions; quantiles are computed at query time with `histogram_quantile()`.
- **Summary** — quantiles calculated client-side. Cheaper to query but **cannot be aggregated across instances**, which is usually disqualifying.

The practical guidance: **prefer histograms to summaries** for latency, because you almost always want a p99 across the whole fleet rather than per-instance. And remember a counter's value alone is meaningless — the rate of change is the signal.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="obsv-prom-03" number={3} question="What is cardinality, and why does it break Prometheus?" difficulty="hard" topic="Prometheus">
Cardinality is the number of distinct time series, and each unique **combination of label values** creates one.

A metric with a `user_id` label and a million users creates a million series. Prometheus holds series metadata in memory, so high cardinality causes memory exhaustion, slow queries and eventually an unusable server — the single most common way people break a Prometheus deployment.

Rules that prevent it:
- **Never label with unbounded values** — user IDs, request IDs, email addresses, full URLs, timestamps, or a raw error message.
- **Keep labels to bounded sets** — environment, service, status code, pipeline name.
- **Put high-cardinality detail in logs or traces**, not metrics. That's what those systems are for.

The related discipline for data pipelines: label by `pipeline` and `table`, not by partition or file name.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="obsv-prom-04" number={4} question="What would you monitor with Prometheus in a data platform?" difficulty="medium" topic="Prometheus">
Beyond the usual CPU and memory:

```text
# ingestion throughput
rate(pipeline_rows_processed_total[5m])

# error rate as a proportion
rate(pipeline_errors_total[5m]) / rate(pipeline_rows_processed_total[5m])

# freshness — how long since a table was last written
time() - pipeline_last_success_timestamp_seconds

# consumer lag
kafka_consumergroup_lag

# p99 task duration
histogram_quantile(0.99, rate(task_duration_seconds_bucket[10m]))
```

The **freshness** metric is the most valuable and the most often missing: exporting a `last_success_timestamp` gauge per dataset means you can alert on "orders hasn't updated in 3 hours" — which catches the silent failures a job-exit-code alert never will.

Alertmanager handles routing, grouping, silencing and deduplication of the resulting alerts.
</QuestionCard>

</QuestionList>

## Commercial Observability Platforms

<QuestionList topic="Commercial Observability Platforms" storagePrefix="observability-platforms">

<QuestionCard id="obsv-plat-01" number={1} question="What is Datadog and when would you choose it over Prometheus?" difficulty="medium" topic="Datadog">
A hosted, unified observability SaaS covering infrastructure metrics, APM and distributed tracing, log management, synthetics, RUM and security — all correlated in one UI, with 800+ integrations.

Choose **Datadog** when you want breadth without operating it: metrics, logs and traces already joined, long retention handled, and no Prometheus, Thanos, Loki, Jaeger and Grafana stack to run yourself. For a small team, the operational saving is real.

Choose **Prometheus + Grafana** when cost at scale matters (Datadog's per-host and per-custom-metric pricing escalates sharply), when you want no vendor lock-in, or when you're already deep in Kubernetes where Prometheus is the native fit.

The honest framing: it's **cost versus operational burden**. Datadog bills are a recurring source of surprise — particularly **custom metrics**, where high-cardinality tags multiply the count and therefore the bill, the same cardinality discipline as Prometheus but with a direct financial signal.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="obsv-plat-02" number={2} question="What is Sentry, and what does it add over logging?" difficulty="medium" topic="Sentry">
An **error tracking** platform. Instead of errors being lines in a log, Sentry captures the exception with its stack trace, local variables, breadcrumbs leading up to it, release version and environment — then **groups identical errors into a single issue** with a count and a trend.

That grouping is the point. A pipeline throwing the same `KeyError` 40,000 times is one issue with a count, not 40,000 log lines you have to notice among everything else.

For data engineering it's most useful in **long-running workers and services** — a streaming consumer, an API — where you want to know about a new exception type immediately, with the context to fix it, and to see whether a deploy introduced it (Sentry associates issues with releases).

It complements logs rather than replacing them: logs for the narrative, Sentry for "what's broken and how often".
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="obsv-plat-03" number={3} question="What is New Relic, and how do these platforms overlap?" difficulty="easy" topic="New Relic">
A full-stack observability platform in the same category as Datadog — APM, infrastructure monitoring, logs, distributed tracing and dashboards, queried with **NRQL**. Its pricing is per-user plus per-GB ingested, which suits different shapes of organisation than Datadog's per-host model.

The honest answer to how they overlap: **substantially.** Datadog, New Relic, Dynatrace, Grafana Cloud and Splunk Observability all sell essentially the same three pillars — metrics, logs and traces — correlated in one place. Differentiation is in pricing model, UI, integration depth and specific strengths rather than in capability.

The interview-relevant point isn't which vendor, it's **what you'd instrument and why**: the four data-specific signals (freshness, volume, schema, quality) that generic APM tools don't cover unless you emit them yourself.
</QuestionCard>

<QuestionCard id="obsv-plat-04" number={4} question="What are the three pillars of observability, and what's missing for data?" difficulty="medium" topic="Fundamentals">
The conventional three:
- **Metrics** — numeric time series, cheap to store, good for alerting and trends. No detail about any individual event.
- **Logs** — discrete events with full context. High detail, high volume, expensive at scale.
- **Traces** — a request's path across services, with timing per span. Shows *where* latency comes from in a distributed call.

What's missing for data pipelines is that all three describe the **system**, not the **data**. A pipeline can be green on every one of them while writing wrong numbers.

So data observability adds a fourth concern: **dataset-level signals** — freshness, volume, schema and quality — plus **lineage** to establish blast radius. That's the distinction worth making in an interview, because it explains why tools like Monte Carlo, Soda and Great Expectations exist alongside Datadog rather than being replaced by it.
</QuestionCard>

</QuestionList>

## Common Interview Scenarios

- **"Freshness on a critical table breaches SLA at 3am — walk through your triage steps."** Tests signal literacy, runbook use, and escalation instincts.
- **"A BI dashboard shows revenue dropped 15% but nothing alerted — how did that slip through, and how do you prevent it next time?"** Tests silent-failure detection and multi-signal alerting.
- **"You need to backfill six months of a core fact table without breaking downstream dashboards — how do you approach it?"** Tests safe backfill strategy and raw/published layer separation.
- **"Design the logging and lineage you'd add to a new pipeline so the next on-call engineer isn't starting from zero."** Tests logging minimums and the practical value of lineage.
